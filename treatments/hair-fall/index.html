Is Your Hair Fall Normal? Understanding the Hair Growth Cycle

Everyone sheds hair every day. Losing roughly 50 to 100 hairs a day is generally considered normal, and some people lose a little more on wash days. Each hair follows a cycle, and hair fall becomes a concern when more hairs than usual enter the resting phase at the same time, or when new hairs grow back thinner or stop growing.

1. Anagen: growthLasts years (about 2 to 8). Most of your hair is in this phase.
2. Catagen: transitionA few weeks (about 2 to 6).
3. Telogen: rest and shedA few months (about 2 to 4), then the hair falls and a new one begins.

Durations are approximate and not to scale. They vary from person to person.

Your hair fall may be more than normal if you notice a widening parting, a thinner ponytail, a changing hairline, bald patches, scalp itching or soreness, or many hairs coming out with a gentle tug. If this continues for more than a few weeks, it is worth getting your scalp examined.

Types of Hair Loss We See at Our Lucknow Clinic

“Hair fall” is not one condition. Each type has a different cause and a different outlook, which is why the pattern of your hair loss matters more than the amount.

Male Pattern Hair Loss (Androgenetic Alopecia)

Gradual thinning at the temples, which can form an "M" shaped hairline, and at the crown. It runs in families and is driven by hormones: the follicles slowly shrink and grow finer, shorter hairs. It usually starts after puberty and progresses slowly.

When to get it checkedSee a doctor early if thinning starts in your teens or twenties, is moving quickly, or comes with an itchy or painful scalp.

Female Pattern Hair Thinning

A widening parting and a thinner ponytail, usually with the frontal hairline kept. Genetics play a role, and hormonal changes such as PCOD or menopause can add to it. Thyroid and iron levels are worth checking.

When to get it checkedSee a doctor if thinning is steady for more than a few months or comes with irregular periods, acne or excess facial hair.

Sudden Diffuse Shedding (Telogen Effluvium)

Hair comes out in handfuls while combing or washing, from all over the scalp, usually two to three months after a trigger such as high fever, surgery, childbirth, a crash diet, severe stress or certain medicines. It is often temporary.

When to get it checkedSee a doctor if shedding lasts longer than about six months, or comes with fatigue, weight change or other symptoms.

Patchy Hair Loss (Alopecia Areata)

Smooth, round, coin-sized bald patches that can appear suddenly on the scalp or beard. It is an autoimmune condition and is sometimes linked to thyroid problems. Hair may regrow, but patches can return.

When to get it checkedSee a doctor promptly for any new patch, especially if more patches appear quickly or eyebrows and eyelashes are affected.

Traction Alopecia (Tight Hairstyles)

Thinning along the hairline and temples from constant pulling by tight ponytails, braids, buns, extensions or headgear. It is reversible if you change the styling early, but long-term pulling can damage follicles permanently.

When to get it checkedSee a doctor if you notice small bumps, pain or redness along the hairline, or if the hairline keeps receding.

Dandruff and Scalp-Related Hair Fall

A flaky, itchy or oily scalp (seborrheic dermatitis, sometimes a fungal infection) can make hair weaker and increase shedding. Scratching and harsh products make it worse. See our page on homeopathic skin treatment for related scalp conditions.

When to get it checkedSee a doctor if the scalp is red, oozing or painful, or if there are round scaly patches with broken hair, which may be a fungal infection needing medical treatment.

Postpartum Hair Fall

Many women shed a lot of hair two to four months after delivery. During pregnancy, hormones keep more hairs in the growth phase, and after birth many enter the resting phase together. Most women see recovery over the following months.

When to get it checkedSee a doctor if shedding is severe, lasts beyond a year, or comes with fatigue, mood changes or other thyroid symptoms.

Thyroid and Hormonal Hair Loss

Underactive or overactive thyroid, PCOD and other hormonal changes can cause diffuse thinning, often with dry, brittle hair. Look for fatigue, weight change, feeling unusually cold or hot, or irregular periods.

When to get it checkedA simple TSH blood test can check the thyroid. Please get tests done if you have these symptoms.

Less common types that need early medical attention

  • Scarring (cicatricial) alopecia: inflammation destroys follicles, so loss can be permanent. A red, painful, burning or scaly scalp should be examined early.
  • Anagen effluvium: rapid loss during chemotherapy or radiation.
  • Trichotillomania: hair loss from the urge to pull hair, which benefits from counselling and support.
  • Tinea capitis: a fungal infection of the scalp, mostly in children, needing medical treatment.

Causes of Hair Fall

More than one cause often works together. Finding the main trigger is the first step of any treatment plan.

Genes and hormones

Pattern hair loss runs in families and depends on hormone sensitivity of the follicles. Thyroid disorders, PCOD, menopause and pregnancy can all change hair growth.

Nutrition

Hair needs protein, iron, zinc and vitamins to grow. Low iron, low protein intake, low vitamin D or B12, and crash dieting are common contributors to shedding.

Stress, sleep and illness

High fever, infections, surgery, long-term stress and poor sleep can push many hairs into the resting phase together, so shedding shows up weeks to months later.

Medicines and treatments

Chemotherapy, some blood thinners, certain antidepressants, retinoids and other medicines can increase shedding. Never stop a prescribed medicine without asking your doctor.

Scalp conditions

Dandruff, seborrheic dermatitis, fungal infections and scalp psoriasis can inflame the scalp and weaken hair. Scarring conditions need early specialist care.

Hair-care habits

Tight hairstyles, daily heat styling, frequent bleaching or straightening and rough towel-drying cause breakage. Hard water, dust and pollution can leave hair dry and brittle, which adds to breakage.

Two causes deserve a special mention. Thyroid problems commonly cause diffuse hair thinning, so read our guide on hypothyroidism and homeopathy. In women, hormonal conditions such as PCOD can also affect hair and skin; see our guide on PCOS and PCOD.

Tests That May Help

Depending on your history, the doctor may suggest tests to rule out a treatable cause. Commonly these include:

  • Thyroid test (TSH)
  • Blood count and ferritin (iron stores)
  • Vitamin D and vitamin B12 levels
  • Hormone tests in women with irregular periods, acne or excess hair growth
  • A scalp scraping or referral for a dermatologist's opinion when a fungal infection or scarring condition is suspected

Tests are suggested only when your history calls for them. Please bring any recent reports.

Hair Fall Treatment: Frequently Asked Questions

How do I stop my hair falling?

Start by finding out why it is falling, because the right step depends on the cause. Common triggers are low iron or vitamin levels, thyroid problems, hormonal changes (PCOD, childbirth, menopause), stress, illness, scalp conditions such as dandruff, and tight or heat-heavy hairstyling. Eat enough protein, handle wet hair gently, avoid tight hairstyles, sleep well and manage stress. If shedding has lasted more than two to three months, get basic blood tests done and have your scalp examined. Pattern hair loss from genes is gradual and usually needs long-term care rather than a quick fix. See a doctor sooner if you notice bald patches, a painful or scaly scalp or sudden heavy shedding.

Can a lost hair grow back?

It depends on the type of hair loss. In shedding caused by a temporary trigger such as illness, childbirth, a crash diet or a stressful period, the follicles are usually still alive, and hair often grows back over several months once the cause is addressed. Patchy loss (alopecia areata) can regrow, sometimes without treatment, but it can come back. In pattern hair loss the follicles shrink gradually, so early care matters for keeping the hair you have. In scarring alopecias the follicles are destroyed and the loss can be permanent, which is why a red, painful, scaly or burning scalp should be examined early. Hair grows roughly 1 cm a month, so changes take months to see.

What is the best treatment for falling hair?

There is no single best treatment, because hair fall covers several different conditions. The best results come from treating the cause: correcting a nutrient deficiency, managing thyroid or hormonal problems, treating a scalp infection or changing hair habits. For pattern hair loss, dermatology options such as minoxidil (and finasteride for men) are commonly used under a doctor's guidance. Homeopathic care is a complementary option. Dr. Jain takes a detailed history covering hair pattern, scalp, stress, sleep, digestion, hormones and family history, and prescribes individually. Response varies, so a proper assessment comes first.

What is the best shampoo for hair loss?

No shampoo can stop hair loss on its own, because shampoo works on the scalp surface and does not change the underlying cause. Choose a mild shampoo that suits your scalp type, wash as often as your scalp needs, and apply conditioner to the lengths rather than the scalp. Washing does not cause hair loss; it only removes hairs that had already shed. If you have dandruff or an itchy, flaky scalp, a medicated anti-dandruff shampoo recommended by your doctor may help the scalp condition, which can reduce shedding linked to it. Avoid very hot water and harsh scrubbing, and check with your doctor before using medicated shampoos for long periods.

How to stop hair fall immediately?

No method stops hair fall overnight. Hairs that shed today are often linked to something that happened weeks or months earlier. What you can do right away is reduce further damage: handle hair gently, avoid tight hairstyles, heat and chemical treatments, do not brush wet hair roughly, eat proper meals with enough protein, sleep well and avoid crash diets or unsupervised supplements. Then book an assessment to find the cause. Seek prompt medical advice if you notice sudden patchy loss, a painful scalp, or hair fall together with fatigue, weight change, irregular periods or other symptoms.

Which food stops hair fall?

No food stops hair fall by itself, but hair needs steady nutrition, and low protein, iron, zinc or vitamin levels can add to shedding. A balanced Indian plate can include protein (dal, chana, rajma, paneer, curd, eggs, fish, chicken, sprouts), iron-rich foods (green leafy vegetables, beetroot, dates, jaggery, pumpkin and sesame seeds) eaten with vitamin C foods such as amla, lemon or guava for better absorption, and nuts, seeds and whole grains for zinc and healthy fats. Sunlight, dairy and eggs support vitamin D and B12; vegetarians may need a B12 test. Supplements are most useful when a test shows a deficiency, and too much of some (vitamin A, selenium, iron, zinc) can itself cause problems, so take them only on medical advice.

How to regrow hair?

Regrowth depends on the type of hair loss and how early it is assessed. The steps that matter are: find and treat the cause, protect the scalp and hair from damage, improve nutrition, sleep and stress, and be patient. Hair grows roughly 1 cm a month, and it often takes three to six months to see a visible change. Conventional options for some types include minoxidil and procedures such as PRP, offered by dermatologists. How many hairs fall per day is normal?

Losing about 50 to 100 hairs a day is generally considered normal, and some sources allow up to about 150, especially on wash days. Each hair goes through a growth phase that lasts years, a short transition phase of a few weeks and a resting phase of a few months before it sheds and is replaced. Shedding may be more than normal if you notice a widening parting, a thinner ponytail, bald patches, a changing hairline or many hairs coming out with a gentle tug. If this lasts more than a few weeks, get it checked.

Does thyroid cause hair fall?

Yes. Both an underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) and an overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism) can cause diffuse thinning, often with dry, brittle hair. Other signs include fatigue, weight change, feeling unusually cold or hot, constipation and irregular periods. A simple TSH blood test can check this. If thyroid disease is confirmed, treating it is essential, and hair usually needs time to recover afterwards.

Is hair fall after delivery normal?

Yes, shedding a few months after childbirth is very common. During pregnancy, hormone levels keep more hairs in the growth phase. After delivery many of them move into the resting phase together and shed, usually peaking around three to four months after birth. For most women hair density gradually recovers over the following months. Good nutrition, rest and the vitamins your doctor advises can help. See a doctor if the shedding is severe, continues beyond about a year, or comes with fatigue, mood changes or other thyroid symptoms, because postpartum thyroid problems can also cause hair loss.
